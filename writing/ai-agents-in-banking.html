What gets an AI agent approved at a bank

Anyone else been to the Singapore FinTech Festival this week? 🙋🏻‍♀️

Did you happen to see the seemingly endless green Sierra ads?

They definitely got my attention. Sierra builds AI agents that talk to customers on behalf of companies, so I was curious what it wanted to tell a festival full of banks, insurers, and other financial institutions.

I missed CEO Bret Taylor’s session, so I looked up what he shared.

According to the festival’s recap, he acknowledged one of the hardest things for banks to accept about AI. It won’t always get it right.

That surprised me. If you’re selling AI, the obvious pitch is how accurate it is. He started from the opposite end.

The more I thought about it, the more it made sense. Banks have never assumed zero errors. They even set aside capital for losses from failed processes, people, and systems, which regulators call operational risk. Mistakes are rare because banks design around them. For a sensitive transaction, one person enters it and another approves it. Limits cap what anyone can do alone. Reviews catch what slips through.

So the questions a bank’s risk team asks about an AI agent may be less “how accurate is it?” and more “when it’s wrong, how quickly do we find out, how much damage can it do, and how do we put it right?”

And buying the AI from a vendor doesn’t take those questions off the bank’s plate. Last month, MAS made this explicit in its new AI risk management guidelines.

Taken together, the product an AI company sells to a bank is more than the intelligence of the agent. It also includes the controls that allow the bank to stand behind it.

That means limits on what the agent can do, a record of what it did, and human review for the cases that need it.

You can see this in how Sierra describes its safeguards.

Tools that can change a customer’s account aren’t even available to the agent until the customer’s identity is verified, and that’s enforced by fixed software checks, not the AI’s judgement. Separate AI supervisors review each response before the customer sees it, and can redirect the conversation or hand it to a person. Monitoring then flags conversations where something still slipped through.

None of this promises the agent won’t make mistakes. It reduces how much depends on the agent being right every time.

So in banking, accuracy may be what gets an AI agent considered. What gets it approved is a clear answer to what happens when it’s wrong.

If you work at a bank or insurer, what slows an AI project down more, getting the AI to work, or getting it approved?
