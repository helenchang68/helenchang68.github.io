What an enterprise actually buys from ElevenLabs

A comment on my last ElevenLabs post raised a question I thought was worth looking into further.

What is an enterprise actually buying when it chooses ElevenLabs?

Looking more closely, the answer seems surprisingly modular.

A company can use ElevenLabs at different levels of the voice-agent stack.

At the lightest level, if a company already has an application that generates the response text, it can use ElevenLabs’ speech APIs to turn that text into speech inside its own product.

If the company already has its own LLM, agent logic, and business tools, Speech Engine can provide the real-time voice interface, including speech-to-text, turn-taking, interruptions, and text-to-speech. The company still owns the reasoning, workflow, and tool execution.

If it wants more of the agent environment provided, it can use ElevenAgents, which brings together the voice layer with knowledge bases, tools, testing, monitoring, and LLM configuration.

Even then, the LLM does not have to come from ElevenLabs. ElevenAgents can use models from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google, as well as a company’s own model.

This is what I found interesting. ElevenLabs and OpenAI do not necessarily sit on opposite sides of the buying decision. They can be part of the same stack.

At a high level, ElevenAgents currently works something like this:

  1. Customer speaks
  2. Speech-to-text converts the audio into text
  3. An LLM processes the text and decides what to do
  4. Tools or APIs can retrieve information or take action
  5. Text-to-speech turns the response back into audio

This is different from a native speech-to-speech voice model, which can process audio directly without relying on a text transcript between the speech and reasoning stages.

That raised another question for me. Why use a cascaded architecture at all?

One advantage is modularity. The individual components are easier to inspect, swap, and connect to enterprise systems, while customers retain more choice over the LLM and surrounding workflow.

Native speech-to-speech may preserve more nuance in human speech, but a modular stack can offer advantages in model choice, observability, integration, and control. ElevenLabs also partially addresses the nuance issue by using real-time speech signals, such as prosody and emotional cues, for turn-taking.

So the question I came away with is really about where an enterprise wants to draw the boundary between what it builds itself and what it buys as part of the platform.

This is a simplified view based on ElevenLabs’ public documentation. I’d be interested to hear from people building voice agents if there are nuances I’ve missed.
