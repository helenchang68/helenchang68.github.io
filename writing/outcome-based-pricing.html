What counts as a successful outcome?

While looking into how AI agent companies price their products, I came across outcome-based pricing.

Instead of charging customers based on how much AI they consume, vendors charge for a defined result.

It made me wonder how vendors set a price when the cost of delivering that result can be so unpredictable.

A study featured by Stanford's Digital Economy Lab found that repeated runs of the same coding task could vary by up to 30x in token consumption. The models also struggled to predict their own usage.

For companies building agents using usage-priced LLM APIs, that uncertainty makes budgeting difficult. Under an outcome-based model for managed AI services, more of the delivery-cost risk shifts to the vendor.

Sierra argues in its article 'Outcomemaxxing' that "outcome-based pricing rewires your company."

Instead of being incentivized to drive more usage, the vendor has a financial incentive to deliver results efficiently.

Intercom's Fin offers a concrete example, charging $0.99 for a customer support resolution, regardless of how many actions the agent takes within the conversation.

But Fin's pricing documentation made me wonder what actually counts as a successful resolution.

A conversation can count as resolved when a customer leaves after receiving an answer without requesting further help. If the customer returns to the same conversation seeking more help, the original charge is reversed, so the conversation isn't billed twice.

Even with this safeguard, a customer leaving doesn't necessarily mean their problem was solved.

So while outcome-based pricing can make the price per outcome more predictable for customers, vendors still need to manage unpredictable delivery costs.

Just as importantly, both sides need to agree on what actually counts as a successful outcome.

If you've worked with outcome-based pricing, I'd be interested to hear more about how vendors and customers navigate this in practice.
