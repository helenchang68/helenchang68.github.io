Why Asia isn't one market for AI

“The only thing that ties our markets together is geography. Very little else.”

Alexander Kleinberg, Wonderful’s General Manager for Singapore, said this about Asia during a panel at Tech Week Singapore 2026. What resonated with me was not just the line, but the explanation that followed.

He described a common playbook among Western companies expanding into Asia. They send a “landing team” to Singapore, then serve the region from there. His example was teams coming from the US, finding it relatively easy to operate in English, and figuring out the rest of Asia as they go.

But the market differences he highlighted go beyond language and localization. They include adoption pace, procurement, and willingness to pay.

For me, this raises a practical GTM question: what made customers say yes in one market, and will those same things matter in the next?

For example, a convincing business case might get a customer in one market to agree to a pilot. A customer in another might first need a local customer reference and confidence that implementation support is available. Understanding that difference changes the sales approach and the partnerships worth building, even when the product is the same.

But getting a customer to say yes also means understanding the different people involved. That is where another of Alex’s comments, this time about leadership, felt relevant.

“Too many leaders talk about what they’re doing. Not enough talk about the why.”

He was discussing how leaders bring their teams along with AI transformation. I think the same question matters when selling an AI product. Why should each person involved care?

The person approving the purchase, the team implementing it, and the employees expected to use it may have different answers.

Leadership might focus on productivity. The implementation team needs to understand how it fits into existing systems. Employees may want to know which tasks become easier and how their roles could change.

An organization’s reason for buying does not automatically give everyone a reason to adopt.

That is why a third line stayed with me.

“The underlying technology is the enabler. It’s not the point.”

Across markets and within organizations, we cannot assume everyone sees the same value in the same product.

What I find particularly interesting about AI GTM and partnerships is understanding what customers need to buy and actually use a product.

Sometimes that means a different business case. Sometimes it means local implementation support. A strong demo alone does not answer those questions.
