Below the surface of the AI stack

I realized recently that most of my exposure to AI has been on the software and research side.

I was already familiar with NVIDIA, AMD, the hyperscalers, and the broader infrastructure story behind AI. But at Tech Week Singapore last week, I was surprised by how much larger the ecosystem was than what I normally see in day-to-day conversations around AI.

There were some applications, but much more around cloud platforms, servers, chips, networking, data centers, cooling and power.

It felt like getting a glimpse below the surface of an iceberg.

After the event, I started mapping some of the companies I had seen. A very rough version of the stack looked like this:

  1. AI applications and agents
  2. Models and developer platforms
  3. Data and software infrastructure
  4. Cloud and AI compute
  5. Servers, networking and storage
  6. Chips and accelerators
  7. Data centers, power and cooling

What became more interesting was how GTM starts to change across those layers.

NVIDIA was the obvious reference point. We associate it most with compute, but its agreed acquisition of Hugging Face would deepen its position in the model and developer ecosystem.

Then there was FuriosaAI, which builds AI inference accelerators. I met Addison Chi, who leads APAC sales from Singapore and kindly walked me through their technology.

I also asked about the pre-sales role they were hiring for, and what stood out was how technical the role is. The sales process can involve benchmarking the platform against a customer's own workloads, testing compatibility and proving the performance and economics before moving into production. Partners can also be part of the route to market, with Samsung SDS offering Furiosa-powered compute through its cloud.

At the cloud and AI compute layer, Bitdeer AI looks different again. I found it helpful to think of it as closer to the GPU-compute part of Google Cloud. For Bitdeer, GTM involves available compute, power, data center capacity and long-term customer commitments.

Then ClickHouse showed me another type of ecosystem. It is a database built to analyze huge volumes of data quickly. Mercado Libre, for example, uses ClickHouse Cloud to investigate questions such as why a payment failed, which can require searching enormous volumes of telemetry and system data almost instantly. Cloud, technology and consulting partners can then help enterprises integrate and deploy it.

At the bottom of the stack, “local” becomes even more literal. Power, cooling, connectivity, land and permits can determine where capacity can be built at all.

The further I looked, the less “AI GTM” looked like a straight line from vendor to customer. It started looking much more like an ecosystem.

Now I find myself looking at an AI company and asking not only

“What does it build?”

but also

“Where does it sit in the stack, who buys from it, and which partners matter in bringing its solution to market?”
