Physical AI: start with tasks, not jobs

I still remember watching a video of a visually impaired person using a robotic guide dog. It was one of those moments that made me appreciate how technology could make meaningful assistance more accessible to people who need it.

More recently, I was watching the Korean drama Between Steps (연애박사), which features a robotics research lab. It reminded me of how advanced South Korea's industrial robotics sector has become. According to the International Federation of Robotics, Korea ranked first globally in industrial robot density in 2024, with 1,220 robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees.

These observations got me thinking more about Physical AI, particularly what it would take to bring its potential into everyday operations.

While LLMs can learn from vast amounts of online text, Physical AI also needs data about how objects behave and interact in the real world. A robot must learn not only to recognize an object, but also how firmly to grasp it, how it responds to movement, and how to adjust when something unexpected happens.

Collecting this data is expensive and time-consuming. Simulation can help, but differences between simulated and real environments still make reliable deployment difficult.

This also creates an interesting commercial challenge.

Industrial robots are already widely used, but more general-purpose Physical AI systems are still relatively early in their development. A successful demonstration doesn't necessarily mean a robot can reliably handle different objects, environments, and unexpected situations during everyday operations.

From a go-to-market perspective, I wonder if the most practical approach is to start with specific tasks rather than entire jobs.

For example, in manufacturing or logistics, companies could prioritize tasks that are repetitive, physically demanding, difficult to staff, or hazardous. The next step would be to assess technical feasibility, collect operational data, and test whether the solution can deliver measurable improvements in safety, productivity, or costs.

With the right data collection and feedback processes, these initial deployments could also help improve the models, potentially making more complex applications possible over time.

What I find particularly interesting is how closely technology development and commercialization are connected in Physical AI. Industrial partnerships aren't just potential sales opportunities. They can also be important sources of the data needed to make the technology better.

I'm still learning about this space and would love to hear from people working in Physical AI, whether in research, robotics development, or commercial deployment. If you have experiences, perspectives, or interesting developments to share, I'd be very interested to learn from them.
